# Constructive framing casebook

These examples show decisions, not replacement formulas. Match the function and context of the user's prose rather than copying the wording.

## Apply

### Needlessly hostile criticism

**Before:** “The team made another stupid decision and ignored the obvious answer.”

**After:** “The team chose an approach that repeated the earlier problem and did not address the clearest alternative.”

**Why:** The revision preserves criticism and responsibility while replacing contempt with specifics.

### Unsupported defeatism

**Before:** “We are never going to recover from this delay.”

**After:** “The delay puts the deadline at risk, so we need to reduce scope or move the date.”

**Why:** The revision keeps the consequence and introduces only choices already available in the situation.

### Discouraging feedback

**Before:** “Your presentation was confusing and failed to make the point.”

**After:** “The central point was difficult to follow; state it at the start and use each section to support it.”

**Why:** The problem remains explicit, but the feedback now gives the recipient a usable direction.

### Aggressive boundary

**Before:** “Stop wasting my time with incomplete requests.”

**After:** “Please include the required details before sending the request so I can act on it.”

**Why:** The boundary and requirement survive without the personal attack.

### Honest encouragement

**Before:** “You still have a long way to go.”

**After:** “You have resolved the first two issues; the remaining work is the data migration and release check.”

**Why:** The revision uses demonstrated progress while keeping the remaining work visible.

## Preserve

### Safety warning

**Source:** “This configuration can permanently delete customer data. Do not run it in production.”

**Decision:** Preserve. Softer language would weaken a necessary warning.

### Grief

**Source:** “I am angry that she died, and I do not want to search for a lesson in it.”

**Decision:** Preserve. A positive lesson would contradict the speaker's emotion and stated intent.

### Accountability

**Source:** “The company concealed the breach for six weeks and exposed customers to avoidable harm.”

**Decision:** Preserve. Reframing this as an opportunity to improve would obscure misconduct and its consequences.

### Firm refusal

**Source:** “No. We will not publish personal information without consent.”

**Decision:** Preserve. Turning the refusal into softer possibility would weaken the boundary.

### Technical diagnosis

**Source:** “The process fails when the token expires.”

**Decision:** Preserve. “Fails” is precise technical language, not a negative attitude.

### Fictional voice

**Source:** “He hated the bright little town and everyone foolish enough to call it home.”

**Decision:** Preserve when the hostility establishes character, narration, or conflict.

## Ask

### Possible sanitization

**Source:** “Management abandoned us when the factory closed.”

**Question:** “Is ‘abandoned’ an intentional statement about management's responsibility, or would you like a more neutral description of what support was withheld?”

**Risk:** A constructive rewrite could erase responsibility or the speaker's lived experience.

### Possible intentional bluntness

**Source:** “The launch was a disaster.”

**Question:** “Should this retain its blunt verdict, or should I replace it with the specific launch failures and consequences?”

**Risk:** The wording may be empty exaggeration or an important voice choice; context decides.

### Possible satire

**Source:** “Another visionary update: now the button does nothing twice as fast.”

**Question:** “Should I preserve the sarcasm, or is the goal a direct product critique?”

**Risk:** Constructive wording would remove the joke and change the rhetorical strategy.

### Possible high-stakes reassurance

**Source:** “The treatment may not work.”

**Question:** “Do you want this to remain an unqualified risk statement, or does the surrounding evidence support adding the available options or next steps?”

**Risk:** Reassurance could distort medical uncertainty.

## Forced positivity to reject

Reject transformations that:

- rename a failure as a success or “learning opportunity” without evidence;
- praise resilience in response to harm without the affected person's invitation;
- add “fortunately,” “at least,” or “the good news is” to minimize loss;
- convert criticism into vague appreciation;
- replace accountability with passive language;
- promise improvement, recovery, opportunity, or growth the facts do not support;
- sound like motivational, therapeutic, or corporate language foreign to the writer;
- attach a cheerful conclusion merely to end on a positive note.

The absence of a constructive opportunity is a valid result. Leave strong, accurate negative prose alone.
