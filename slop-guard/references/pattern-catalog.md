# Pattern catalog

Use this catalog to locate editorial problems. A match is not automatically a defect, and no match proves that prose is good or human-written. Judge the effect on the specific piece.

## 1. Unsupported authority

### Invented specificity

The prose supplies names, numbers, examples, quotations, mechanisms, or outcomes that the source never established.

**Signals:** unexplained statistics, suspiciously precise improvements, fabricated customer stories, or concrete product behavior absent from the brief.

**Test:** Can every detail be traced to the user, source material, or verified research?

**Fix:** Restore the supported claim, mark a placeholder, or ask for the missing fact.

### Weasel attribution

Claims borrow credibility from unnamed authorities: “research shows,” “experts believe,” “many teams report.”

**Test:** Who said this, where, and based on what evidence?

**Fix:** Name the source and scope or remove the borrowed authority.

### Inflated certainty and causality

The prose turns correlation into cause, possibility into certainty, or one example into a universal claim.

**Signals:** guarantees, inevitable outcomes, sweeping “always” or “everyone” claims, and causal verbs unsupported by the evidence.

**Fix:** Match the claim's strength to the source. Preserve genuine uncertainty rather than sanding it away.

### Empty balance

A passage gestures at “both sides” without naming their evidence, stakes, or asymmetry.

**Fix:** Describe the actual disagreement and evaluate the relevant evidence. Balance is not a substitute for judgment.

## 2. Portable prose

Portable prose could move unchanged to another company, person, product, or topic.

**Signals:** claims about unlocking potential, driving innovation, navigating change, delivering value, or shaping the future without a mechanism or consequence.

**Test:** Replace the subject with an unrelated one. If the sentence still works, it probably carries little information.

**Fix:** Add a supported fact, mechanism, constraint, example, consequence, or judgment specific to the subject. Cut the sentence when none exists.

Do not manufacture details merely to pass this test.

## 3. Generic framing

### Throat-clearing

Openers announce candor, importance, or the act of explanation before reaching the point.

**Signals:** “It is worth noting,” “let's explore,” “when it comes to,” “the reality is,” or a paragraph explaining what the next paragraph will say.

**Fix:** Start with the point unless the setup supplies context, tension, or voice.

### Faux-insight setup

The writer claims privileged understanding before making an ordinary observation.

**Signals:** “what everyone misses,” “the hidden truth,” “few people realize,” or “the surprising part.”

**Fix:** Remove the status claim and let the evidence make the observation interesting.

### Interpretive instructions

The prose tells readers that a detail is important, revealing, remarkable, nuanced, or symbolic instead of showing its consequence.

**Fix:** Supply the missing evidence or consequence. If the surrounding material already establishes the point, delete the instruction.

### Recap ending

The final paragraph repeats the introduction or summarizes points the reader just encountered.

**Fix:** End on the last concrete consequence, decision, implication, or next action that the piece has earned.

## 4. Synthetic emphasis

### Binary correction

A sentence manufactures force through opposition: “The issue is not X. It is Y,” or “not merely X, but Y.”

**Test:** Does the contrast correct a real misconception or distinguish concepts the reader could confuse?

**Fix:** Keep useful distinctions. Otherwise state the stronger claim directly.

### Negative ladder

Several fragments reject alternatives before revealing the preferred label: “Not a feature. Not an add-on. A new foundation.”

**Fix:** Name the thing and explain what makes it so.

### Dramatic reveal

A colon, fragment, or one-line paragraph delays an ordinary fact to imitate suspense.

**Fix:** Integrate the fact into a normal sentence unless the reveal has earned dramatic weight.

### Manufactured profundity

A slogan, aphorism, or metaphor restates the preceding point as a grand conclusion.

**Test:** Does the line add a consequence, decision, or insight, or only a quotable rhythm?

**Fix:** Delete the echo. End on the clearest earned statement.

### Importance inflation

Phrases declare a milestone, transformation, testament, or defining moment without demonstrating scale.

**Fix:** State what happened, what changed, and for whom. Let readers judge its importance.

## 5. Structural defaults

### Template progression

The piece follows a generic sequence regardless of content: broad trend, list of benefits, generic challenges, optimistic conclusion.

**Fix:** Organize around the reader's real questions, the argument's dependencies, or the chronology of the event.

### Excessive decomposition

Every thought receives a heading, every pair of sentences becomes a list, or every concept sits in the same visual container.

**Fix:** Use structure to expose relationships. Merge units that do not need independent navigation.

### Repetition disguised as development

Later paragraphs paraphrase earlier claims without adding evidence, mechanism, qualification, or consequence.

**Test:** What new work does this passage perform?

**Fix:** Cut it or make it advance the reasoning.

### Mechanical completeness

The prose forces ideas into threes, covers symmetrical pros and cons, or adds a final category only to make a list feel complete.

**Fix:** Use the number of items the subject requires.

## 6. Rhythm and syntax

### Repeated sentence molds

Several sentences share the same length, opening, clause order, or ending.

**Signals:** repeated “This means…,” consecutive imperative sentences, or paragraphs built from identical topic-detail-summary units.

**Fix:** Combine, cut, or restructure according to the relationship between ideas. Do not vary sentences randomly.

### Fragment stacks

Short fragments pile up to simulate urgency or confidence.

**Fix:** Restore the logical relationship between the fragments. Keep a fragment when it matches the writer's voice and earns its emphasis.

### Trailing commentary

A sentence ends with a participial clause that labels its own meaning: “The team changed the policy, demonstrating its commitment to trust.”

**Fix:** State the concrete result or remove the interpretation.

### Transition dependence

Paragraphs rely on “moreover,” “additionally,” “in contrast,” or “ultimately” where the relationship should be clear from the ideas.

**Fix:** Remove the transition when the sequence holds without it. Keep transitions that prevent a genuine ambiguity.

### Monotone polish

Every sentence is equally smooth, balanced, and complete. Humor, hesitation, bluntness, and changes in pace disappear.

**Fix:** Restore useful irregularity from the writer's voice. Clarity does not require uniformity.

## 7. Diction

### Inflated verbs

Words such as “leverage,” “utilize,” “facilitate,” “empower,” “elevate,” and “unlock” often conceal the actual action.

**Fix:** Name who does what. Keep the word when it is the precise domain term.

### Prestige adjectives

Words such as “robust,” “transformative,” “cutting-edge,” “seamless,” “dynamic,” and “innovative” assert quality without evidence.

**Fix:** Replace the judgment with a supported property or outcome.

### Vague abstractions

Terms such as “landscape,” “ecosystem,” “journey,” “realm,” “complexity,” and “potential” can blur the subject.

**Fix:** Name the system, process, constraint, or opportunity. Keep established technical meanings.

### Empty modifiers

Words such as “simply,” “truly,” “deeply,” “highly,” “incredibly,” and “fundamentally” may add tone without information.

**Fix:** Remove them when the sentence loses nothing. Preserve deliberate emphasis and natural speech.

### Synonym cycling

The prose rotates labels to avoid repeating the clearest term, making one actor or concept appear to be several.

**Fix:** Repeat the canonical term unless a new label marks a real distinction.

### Fake-strong verbs

An elaborate predicate replaces a clear “is,” “has,” or direct action: “serves as a hub for” instead of naming what the product stores or coordinates.

**Fix:** Prefer the verb that states the actual relationship.

## 8. Formatting habits

### Decorative emphasis

Bold text, italics, emoji, title case, quotation marks, or one-line paragraphs supply importance the prose has not earned.

**Fix:** Keep formatting that supports scanning or meaning. Remove formatting used only to manufacture energy.

### List reflex

Related prose is split into bullets even when order, comparison, or independent scanning does not matter.

**Fix:** Use prose for an argument or narrative relationship. Use a list when readers need to scan distinct items.

### Dash dependence

Frequent em dashes become a default pause, aside, or reveal.

**Fix:** Choose punctuation that reflects the relationship: a period for separation, parentheses for an aside, a colon for an explanation, or a comma for continuity. Keep an em dash when its interruption is deliberate.

## Context checks

Before changing a match, ask:

1. Is it accurate?
2. Does it add information or reveal voice?
3. Is it repeated enough to become noticeable?
4. Is it appropriate for the genre and audience?
5. Would the proposed fix preserve meaning and cadence?

If the current wording passes these checks, leave it alone.
