# Constructive framing decision guide

Use this guide to decide whether positivity improves a passage or falsifies it. The target is constructive accuracy, not elevated sentiment.

## Apply constructive framing

Reframe when the negative wording is optional and a new frame improves the passage without changing what it says or does.

### Replace contempt with a useful distinction

Move from insults, sneering, or dismissal to the specific behavior, limitation, consequence, or disagreement that matters. Keep justified moral clarity.

### Replace unsupported fatalism with available agency

Name a credible next step, boundary, choice, lesson, or open possibility when the source supports one. Agency may be small; do not exaggerate it.

### Replace blame with observable responsibility

Prefer who did what, what followed, and what must change over vague accusation. Keep accountability and power differences explicit.

### Replace problem-only framing with constructive direction

When the purpose calls for action, pair a supported problem with a practical response. Do not bolt a solution onto prose whose purpose is testimony, mourning, warning, or diagnosis.

### Replace discouragement with honest encouragement

Emphasize demonstrated progress, capability, effort, or possibility already present in the facts. Encouragement must be earned by the material.

### Replace hostile delivery with firm clarity

A refusal, correction, boundary, or criticism can remain direct while shedding needless aggression. Firmness is not negativity.

## Preserve necessary negativity

Keep negative framing when it performs essential work.

- **Facts and stakes:** failure, danger, loss, scarcity, uncertainty, and consequences.
- **Warnings and prohibitions:** safety, security, legal, medical, financial, or operational risks.
- **Accountability:** abuse, injustice, negligence, exploitation, misconduct, and unequal power.
- **Legitimate emotion:** grief, anger, fear, disappointment, frustration, and despair.
- **Clear boundaries:** refusals, disqualification, termination, and non-negotiable requirements.
- **Criticism and diagnosis:** defects, poor decisions, unsupported claims, and root causes.
- **Narrative function:** conflict, antagonists, tension, tragedy, satire, irony, and character voice.
- **Source fidelity:** quotations, testimony, historical language, technical terminology, and attributed positions.

Preserving negativity does not require preserving cruelty, vagueness, exaggeration, or avoidable hostility around it.

## Ask before changing

Treat a passage as uncertain when any of these questions cannot be answered confidently:

1. Would the change reduce the apparent severity of harm, risk, or consequences?
2. Would it shift responsibility away from the actor who holds it?
3. Would it tell someone how they should feel about pain or loss?
4. Would it turn a firm boundary into an invitation to negotiate?
5. Would it weaken satire, irony, humor, characterization, or an intentional tonal contrast?
6. Would it alter a quotation, testimony, legal position, technical term, or historical claim?
7. Would it add hope, progress, praise, agency, a lesson, or a solution not established by the material?
8. Could the original bluntness be a deliberate part of the writer's voice?

Leave uncertain wording unchanged. Quote only the relevant passage, explain the possible loss in one sentence, and ask a focused confirmation question.

## The preservation test

Before accepting a reframe, compare it with the source:

- Can a reasonable reader infer the same events and responsibility?
- Do the severity and stakes feel equally real?
- Is the emotional permission unchanged?
- Does the passage still perform the same job?
- Is every new positive implication supported?
- Would the writer recognize the voice?

A single failed answer means preserve the source or ask the user.
